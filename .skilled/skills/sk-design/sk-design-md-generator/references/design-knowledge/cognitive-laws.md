# Cognitive Laws for Interface Design

Eight cognitive laws that give concise, citable rationale for why a surface's hierarchy, interaction, and timing read the way they do. Shared vocabulary, not a complete UX research method.

---

## 1. OVERVIEW

### Purpose

Give a compact, citable rationale set for reading and explaining design choices captured in a Style Reference.

### Usage

Reference a law by name when explaining why a captured hierarchy, interaction, or timing works — or where a surface violates it.

---

## 2. HICK'S LAW

Decision time rises as choices increase. Reduced visible options, grouped related actions, and progressively disclosed advanced choices are marks of a surface that respects this; a wall of equal options is a finding.

---

## 3. MILLER'S LAW

Working memory is limited. Information chunked into meaningful groups reads better than many isolated items compared at once.

---

## 4. FITTS'S LAW

Targets are faster to hit when larger and closer. Primary controls that are easy to acquire — especially on touch and in repeated workflows — show intent.

---

## 5. DOHERTY THRESHOLD

Interfaces feel responsive when feedback arrives quickly enough to preserve flow. Immediate state feedback, optimistic affordances, or progress cues are the marks of a surface that keeps flow intact.

---

## 6. AESTHETIC-USABILITY EFFECT

People often perceive attractive interfaces as easier to use. Visual polish can improve tolerance, but it must not hide broken hierarchy, unclear affordances, or accessibility failures — capture both the polish and what it may be masking.

---

## 7. VON RESTORFF EFFECT

Distinctive items are more memorable. Contrast that makes the one important action stand out is intent; contrast that makes every element louder is noise.

---

## 8. LAW OF PROXIMITY

Items near each other are perceived as related. Spacing that communicates grouping before any line or box is the cleaner signal.

---

## 9. LAW OF COMMON REGION

Items inside the same bounded area are perceived as related. Cards, panels, fieldsets, and background regions clarify relationships when proximity alone is not enough.

---

## 10. HOW THIS SKILL USES IT

Cite these laws when explaining why a captured hierarchy, interaction pattern, or motion timing works, and when a finding in an extracted surface depends on one of them. The law names give a reference concise, defensible rationale instead of unsupported assertion.
